# SME Weak-Spot Inventory — Learning Sciences

> Produced 2026-08-17 by the learning-science SME agent (stage 1 of the two-stage
> panel). Feeds the stage-2 red-team + game-theory pass and the defense Q&A brief.

## Ranking by pedagogical defensibility: C > B > A

## Candidate A — Vocab Lock

**Citation soundness (weakest of the three):**
1. **Krashen inversion (RED LINE):** comprehensible input is *i+1* — input slightly ABOVE current competence. A 1,000-word ceiling is i or i−1. Citing Krashen for a vocabulary ceiling gets the hypothesis backwards.
2. Wrong domain: Krashen is L2 acquisition of *language itself*; we're teaching STEM *content*, mostly L1 speakers.
3. Krashen's Input Hypothesis is itself criticized as unfalsifiable (Gregg 1984; McLaughlin 1987) — irony hazard.
4. Thing Explainer is a constraint game with zero outcome data — cite as tradition, never as grounding.
5. Nation (2006): real-text comprehension needs ~98% coverage ≈ 8–9k word families — the cap optimizes the wrong artifact.

**Pedagogical validity:**
6. Technical terms are learning objectives (Vygotsky's scientific concepts; Beck, McKeown & Kucan) — a tutor who never says "denominator" produces a student who can't parse the textbook.
7. Lossy paraphrase manufactures misconceptions (Chi 2005, emergent-vs-direct confusions).
8. Fluency illusion: easy prose inflates judged learning without actual learning (Bjork, desirable difficulties).
9. Expertise reversal (Kalyuga et al. 2003): heavy simplification harms intermediate+ learners.

**Strongest correct version:** drop Krashen. *"The tutor explains new concepts in
plain language and introduces each necessary technical term explicitly with a
plain-language gloss, never using an undefined technical term."* Grounding:
plain-language/health-literacy (DeWalt et al. 2004 systematic review) + explicit
vocabulary instruction (Beck et al.). Honest limit: that evidence is about
comprehension/recall, not conceptual mastery.

**Killer grader question:** "Krashen says input must be slightly above the
learner's level; your cap is below it — how is Krashen your grounding?"
Only survivable answer: he isn't; grounding is plain-language research; the cap
is a measurable readability proxy with a defined-term whitelist exception.

## Candidate B — No-Praise

1. **Dweck overshoot (RED LINE):** Mueller & Dweck (1998) contrast person vs process praise and find process praise *beneficial* — a total ban prohibits what the research recommends.
2. Replication dispute is live ammunition: Li & Bates (2019) largely failed to replicate; commentary contests the failure; mindset meta-analyses (Sisk et al. 2018) find weak effects.
3. Montessori "acknowledgment not praise" is philosophy (Kohn, RIE), not controlled evidence.
4. What IS supported: Hattie & Timperley (2007) — self-directed praise is the least effective feedback level; Kluger & DeNisi (1996) — self-attention feedback can reverse learning effects. Supports banning *person-directed* evaluation only.
5. SDT cuts against total abstinence: informational positive feedback enhances intrinsic motivation (Deci, Koestner & Ryan 1999).
6. Expert tutors are not affect-neutral (Lepper & Woolverton INSPIRE).
7. Bare-verdict feedback is the weakest feedback type (Shute 2008).

**Strongest correct version:** *"Never evaluates the person or their ability; all
feedback directed at task, process, or self-regulation."* (Hattie & Timperley levels.)

**Killer question:** "Dweck's own finding is that process praise helps; which
literature says removing ALL praise improves learning?" Honest answer: none —
must own the total ban as a measurability-driven overapproximation.

## Candidate C — Self-Explanation Gate

1. Paradigm mismatch (survivable): canonical self-explanation effect is prompted explanation *during study* (Chi et al. 1989/1994; Bisra et al. 2018 meta-analysis g=0.55) — no study manipulates "no feedback until you explain."
2. **Hypercorrection miscited (RED LINE):** Butterfield & Metcalfe (2001) supports *commit-before-feedback* with feedback DELIVERED — it argues for prompt correction, not withholding.
3. Better adjacent citations: Siegler 2002 / Rittle-Johnson 2006 (explaining right/wrong answers); Graesser's AutoTutor pumps ("how did you get that?"); VanLehn 2011.
4. Feedback-timing objection weak (delay is one turn — answerable).
5. Real failure mode: assistance dilemma (Koedinger & Aleven 2007) — rigid gating of floundering/low-confidence students produces frustration and gaming.
6. Trivial-recall items break the rule (demanding reasoning for "what is 7×8" is pedagogically empty).

**Strongest correct version:** *"Before giving a verdict, the tutor elicits the
student's reasoning, then gives immediate corrective, elaborated feedback keyed
to that reasoning."*

**Killer question:** "Chi's studies are about worked examples — where's the
evidence for withholding feedback on the student's own answer?" Survivable only
by conceding directness is thin and arguing mechanism + one-turn delay.

## Red lines (never claim these)

- Krashen supports a 1,000-word ceiling — wrong (i+1 is above-level; wrong domain).
- "Dweck showed praise harms learning" — wrong (person vs process; contested replication).
- "The self-explanation effect was demonstrated as explain-before-feedback" — wrong (study-time paradigm).
- Hypercorrection justifies withholding feedback — wrong (it's about delivered feedback).
- Thing Explainer as evidence — wrong (no outcome data).
